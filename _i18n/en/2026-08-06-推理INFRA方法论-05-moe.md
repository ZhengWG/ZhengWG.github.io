Follow batch size through expert coverage, communication volume, tile fill, and load imbalance, and check the analytical budget against end-to-end measurements.

## How shapes and sparsity have evolved

| Model | E (routed) | k | Expert intermediate size | Sparsity | Form |
|--------------------------|-----------|--------|-------------|----------|---------------------------|
| Dense FFN (Llama-70B)    | —         | —      | 28672       | 100%     | one large GEMM |
| DeepSeek-V3 (2024)       | 256       | 8+1s   | 2048        | 5.5%     | fine-grained + shared |
| Qwen3.5 (2026)           | 512       | 10+1s  | smaller     | 4.3%     | ultra-fine-grained (shared expert removed, then brought back) |
| DS-V4-Pro (2026)         | 384       | 6+1s   | 2048        | 3.1%     | + native FP4 |

Trend: E up 64×, each expert 14× smaller, sparsity 25%→3%.

Consequence: per-expert GEMMs went from "large and dense" to "small and fragmented" — every MoE systems problem (padding, EP, EPLB) stems from this change in shape.

## MoE through the Roofline lens: how batch size, communication, and compute interact

How batch size affects compute efficiency:

```
┌─→ AI ↑ → throughput ↑ (nearly free before B* for dense models; MoE also multiplies by C(B), see below)
                    │
                    ├─→ M_e ↑ → GEMM pushed from the padding region toward saturation
        B ↑ ────────┤
                    ├─→ all2all bytes ∝ B → communication time ↑ (becomes the new bottleneck across nodes)
                    │
                    └─→ KV pool demand ∝ B → capped by GPU memory (EP unlocks it)

Actual operating point = min( B*, B_SLA, B_max_mem )
                              ↑best throughput  ↑TPOT cap  ↑memory cap
```

B is the only variable that appears on all four chains at once. Every MoE system optimization is, in essence, engineering between "pushing B up" and "removing the side effects of pushing B up" — EP unlocks GPU memory so B can go up, and each side effect (more communication, padding, imbalance) has its own library that treats it.

For MoE, "free before B*" comes with a discount: the fraction of experts hit within one step, `C(B)=1−(1−k/E)^B`, grows with B, and so do the weights read. Let W_routed be the number of routed-expert parameters and W_shared the number of other parameters that must be read every step; weight traffic is then roughly `[C(B)·W_routed+W_shared]·bpp`. An approximately flat region can form only when C(B) is close to 1 and no other limit, such as compute, has been hit yet.

Under ideal conditions — all experts covered, other operators ignored, the same sharding scheme — setting the weight-read time equal to `2·N_active·B/F` gives `B*≈Ridge·W_total·bpp/(2·N_active)`. This is a ridge-point estimate for the weight GEMMs and cannot be equated directly with the throughput-optimal serving batch; the real operating point is also constrained by KV, attention, communication, and the SLO.

| Chain | Symptom | Who treats it | Mechanism |
|---------------------|--------------------------------------|-------------|------------------------------------------------------------|
| all2all bytes ∝ B | cross-node communication becomes the new bottleneck | DeepEP | turns communication from an "additive term" into "a term that compute can hide" |
| M_e falls in the padding region | compute is spent on pad rows, a 10× waste | DeepGEMM | lets static shapes hold dynamic loads, cutting the pad granularity down to the real M_e |
| Uneven expert load | the all2all synchronization barrier → the slowest GPU determines the whole layer | EPLB | spreads out hot experts, pushing max/mean back to 1 |

### End-to-end measurements: keep the theoretical lower bound separate from serving capacity

Qwen3.5-397B-A17B-FP8, 8 GPUs, TP8; input 1024/output 128, radix cache disabled, static memory fraction 0.85, maximum decode graph batch 256. The table below records single-batch samples from `bench_one_batch_server`.

| batch | Graph decode tok/s | TPOT | eager TPOT |
|---|---:|---:|---:|
| 1 | 177 | 5.6 ms | 144 ms |
| 8 | 896 | 8.9 ms | 141 ms |
| 32 | 1948 | 16.4 ms | 146 ms |
| 64 | 2828 | 22.6 ms | 143 ms |
| 128 | 4136 | 30.9 ms | — |
| 256 | 5626 | 45.5 ms | — |

Under a strict 30ms TPOT cap, B=128 at 30.9ms misses the target; among the existing samples, B=64 at 22.6ms meets it, but the maximum throughput that still meets the cap has not been located.

Prefill throughput is about 26K tok/s, with a B=1 TTFT of 74ms. With natural-language prompts, the MTP-3 acceptance length is 3.07–3.11, and TPOT at B=1/8/32 is 2.45/5.1/7.6ms; the acceptance length of 3.64 with random-token prompts is inflated and is not used for conclusions about the gain. Without MTP, B≤8 is already below 10ms, so the claim that "10ms requires 16 GPUs + MTP" does not hold.

The full-weight-read lower bound `397GB / 8 / 3.33TB/s = 14.9ms` assumes the step reads all of the weights; for small batches, use the union of experts hit instead. B=256 at 45.5ms is about 3.1× this ideal lower bound, and the gap may simultaneously include insufficient effective bandwidth, compute, communication, and more, so you cannot subtract to conclude that "other overheads account for 2/3".

### DeepEP



The essence of the communication volume: `dispatch = B·C·d·1B(FP8) + combine = B·k·d·2B(BF16)`, where `C = P[1−(1−1/P)^k]` is the number of distinct destination GPUs after deduplication (7.18 at EP32, not k=8). The bytes grow linearly with B; this term cannot be eliminated, so only three things can be done:

```
① Cut bytes: dispatch in FP8 (halved); multiple experts on the same rank share one copy of the token data (deduplication, C<k)
② Cut α: IBGDA lets the GPU's SMs write NIC WQEs and ring the doorbell directly, bypassing the CPU proxy
        —— cross-node all2all is high-frequency small messages, so α rather than bandwidth is the dominant term
③ ★ Turn the sum into a max: the hook mechanism. Once send is issued, RDMA is executed by the NIC and occupies no SMs;
   compute kernels queue as usual, and the recv hook is called only when the data is needed
   ⟹ TBO (two-batch overlap) builds on this mechanism, turning layer time from t_comm + t_comp into max(·,·)
```

"Occupies no SMs" holds only for cross-node RDMA. Within a node over NVLink, the SMs move the data themselves: measured on EP4 (E=256, k=8, d=7168), low-latency dispatch takes 32–50 μs and combine takes 30–76 μs, and hook overlap hides only 20–27% of the communication time. The measured number of distinct destination GPUs is 3.44–3.61, matching the formula's 3.60.


Two sets of kernels match the two phases: `normal` (prefill, asymmetric-domain forwarding NVLink→RDMA, bandwidth-heavy) / `low-latency` (decode, pure single-hop RDMA + fixed preallocated buffers → capturable by CUDA Graph).

Where its value lies: DeepEP does not reduce communication volume; it takes communication off the critical path. So its maximum gain = communication's share of step time, which can reach 40-60% in cross-node large-EP deployments.

### DeepGEMM

The core tension in MoE decode is two conflicting constraints:

```
Constraint A (CUDA Graph):  shapes must be static —— fixed at capture, cannot change at replay
Constraint B (MoE reality): the real row count per expert, masked_m[e], changes every step and is small
```

Of three approaches, only one satisfies both at once:

| Approach | Graph support | Waste at M_e=5 |
|-----------------------------------|------------|-------------------------------|
| Dense batched GEMM padded to M_max | ✅         | 12.8× (computes all of M_max) |
| CUTLASS grouped (by the real M) | ❌ the shape changes every step | 12.8× (tiles too large) |
| DeepGEMM masked                   | ✅         | 3.2× (BM=16) → 1.6× (BM=8); this is FLOP waste |

The core trick: static memory, dynamic compute. The buffer shape is fixed at `[E_loc, M_max, K]` (meeting the graph requirement), while `masked_m` is a GPU tensor — a fixed address whose contents can change every step — which the kernel reads at runtime to decide how many blocks to actually compute:

```
m_size = __ldg(&masked_m[tile.e]);        // 运行时读该专家真实行数
if (tile.m_blk * BM >= m_size) continue;  // 纯 pad 块:一条 MMA 都不发
```

Two supporting pieces: two-level accumulation solves FP8 precision for deep K (Tensor Cores accumulate only 128 K at a time, then CUDA cores multiply by the scale and promote to FP32; measured with all-positive data at K=16K, the error without promotion is about 25%, versus about 0.17% with two-level accumulation, which equals BF16 output rounding); full JIT specialization can choose the tile by shape. Whether a particular version uses expected_m to change BM has to be measured; here, changing expected_m on the SM90 masked path did not change the actual BM=64. Prefill uses the contiguous layout and larger tiles to amortize weight re-reads.

Why there is still waste even though "pure pad blocks issue no MMA": skipping applies to whole tiles, so as long as a block contains one row of real data, all BM rows are computed, and the FLOP waste = ⌈M_e/BM⌉·BM / M_e. While the tile is still memory-bandwidth-bound, this waste is only FLOPs and costs no time.

> The critical BM is also a Roofline question: a single tile's arithmetic intensity = 2·BM/bpp, independent of the real M_e. BM* = Ridge×bpp/2; with reference FP8 parameters it is about 37, and about 42 once the measured effective throughput is plugged in. Below it, the tile is still bandwidth-bound and pad rows hide inside the weight reads almost for free; above it, the tile flips to compute-bound and pad rows start burning real time. Measured (sglang Triton fused_moe, W8A8 block-128, 8 experts gate_up+down totaling 352 MB, M_e ≤ 16): BM=16/32/64/128 take 118/159/202/445 μs, i.e. 3.0/2.2/1.7/0.8 TB/s, so going from BM 128 to 16 is 3.8× faster; the real kernel starts slowing down at BM≈16–24, earlier than the theoretical ridge point. The SM90 masked path in DeepGEMM 0.2.2 actually uses BM=64 and reaches only 1.8 TB/s at small M_e.

★ The two libraries interlock: DeepEP's recv_count[num_local_experts] is exactly DeepGEMM's masked_m, and the receive buffer serves directly as the GEMM input, with zero copies and zero reordering.

## EPLB

all2all is a synchronization barrier → MoE layer time = the time of the busiest GPU → slower by a factor of `S₀ = max_load/mean_load`. Imbalance has two **independent sources**, and only one of them can be treated:

```
① Statistical fluctuation: S₀ ≈ 1 + √(2lnP) · CV_gpu, where CV_gpu = CV_e/√e (self-averaging over the e experts on a GPU)
   a floor of about ±13% at B_local=48 —— ★ EPLB cannot fix this; only a larger batch helps
② Routing hot spots: S₀ ≈ the hot-to-cold ratio r (with a bad placement)
   ⟹ EPLB replicates hot experts + LPT bin packing spreads them out, pushing S₀ back to ~1.1
```

**Converting to gains** (note the two levels of dilution):

```
MoE layer speedup = S₀/S₁
End to end        = 1 / ( f/(S₀/S₁) + (1−f) )        f = balanceable fraction
             ↑ EPLB balances both the GEMMs and each GPU's a2a receive volume (both ∝ expert load)
               What it cannot fix: total bytes, the α floor, attention
```

| Scenario | e (experts per GPU) | CV_e  | S₀/S₁ | f    | End to end |
| ------------------ | ----------- | ----- | ----- | ---- | --------- |
| EP32, B=48, cross-node | 8           | ~0.35 | 1.18  | 0.6  | **1.10×** |
| SGLang EP72 decode | 4           | ≥1.3  | ~2.6  | 0.85 | **2.54×** |

> **★ EPLB's gain is determined by three factors, and all three are required**: how hot the hot spots are (CV_e), the number of experts per GPU (the smaller e is, the less self-averaging there is), and the balanceable fraction (f). **With e=8 + a large batch + uniform routing, 10% is the ceiling, not an implementation problem**; to see gains on the order of 2×, you need EP64+/e≤4 + real heavy-tailed routing + redundant experts.
